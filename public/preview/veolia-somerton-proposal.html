The case is capacity, not labour

Somerton turned away five trailers in a single week — requests it could not service. Pomona offers two more per week, Fernley one, with further Veolia origins behind them.

The dumper against phase 4 alone

Minutes. The 45-minute bar is one phase of six, not the trailer — a trailer runs about 4.5 hours. The comparison below is phase 4 only, and it is the narrowest reading of the case.

An earlier draft of this analysis compared the dumper's full 33–50 minute cycle against one 45-minute phase and concluded the machine might be slower. That comparison was wrong — it set a whole-machine cycle against a sixth of the work. The proposed equipment touches four of the six phases.

The constraint is trailer unload — and it is the one phase that cannot be automated

The 45-minute figure is a takt: a line-balancing rhythm, not a measured cycle. The phases were balanced to each other's pace. So 4.5 hours is lead time — how long one trailer takes to traverse the line — not throughput. In a balanced line, phases work different trailers at the same time, and output is one trailer per takt.

Every phase downstream of unload can be overhauled, rebalanced or automated. Unload cannot. Only one forklift fits inside a 53 ft trailer at a time. That is a physical fact of the box, not a staffing choice.

The consequence, stated plainly: automating phases 3 through 6 does not raise throughput while unload holds at 45 minutes. A line runs at the speed of its bottleneck. What downstream automation delivers is labour reduction, hazard removal, and the ability to rebalance to a shorter takt — but that gain is only realised once unload moves.

The dual-ramp fix — the limit is drop lanes, not forklifts

An earlier reading of this called unload immovable: one forklift fits inside a 53 ft trailer, so the phase cannot improve. That was wrong. The limit is not how many forklifts fit inside — it is how many drop lanes they share.

A second ramp on the opposite side of the Dockzilla gives each operator its own lane. Two forklifts alternate at the door, one entering while the other places, and neither waits. That is a pipeline, not parallel access.

Trailer spotting absorbs into existing labour: the driver making nine landfill runs per shift has time between them to pull trailers in, and the 53 ft pavement sections stay open to consolidate the load while both ramps work.

Estimated effect: a forklift cycle is roughly 30% inside the trailer and 70% travel-and-place. Overlapping the travel half should take the phase from 45 to 25–30 minutes — short of a clean 2x because of handoff friction at the door. ESTIMATE, requires measurement.

The contractor is the hedge

At roughly $130/hour netting about 10 loads a day, a contracted haul runs near $104 per load — a 13–30% add against a $350–780 tipping fee.

A structural point alongside the arithmetic: it is rented capacity, paid only on days the volume justifies it. That answers the objection that automation builds capacity demand may not fill — everything above the owned truck is variable cost, not capital.

Every roll-off has two ceilings, and one is being wasted

A box is limited by volume and by weight. Containers are currently dumped into whichever box is open, so most boxes cube out while still light, or weigh out with volume to spare. Either way one ceiling is thrown away.

Loads in the observed range carry the same per-trip base fee regardless of how much weight they hold, so the fee per pound varies with fill.

A routing rule would address this without equipment. Classify each container by content type and density — drums, sacks, pallet mixtures — then send dense material to a box that is cubing out, and light fibrous material to one with weight headroom. Both ceilings fill together.

The freed truck-hours also feed straight back into the capacity argument, which makes this the rare item that improves both the cost side and the throughput side at once.

Stated plainly: demand is the ceiling, not supply. Roughly 7 trailers a day today, with 2–5 more per week available. Capacity for 10–12 a day runs ahead of current demand. The case rests on capacity having to exist before volume can be accepted — five trailers were refused in one week precisely because it did not. That is a real argument, and it is not the same as claiming twelve trailers a day are waiting.

Two positions withdrawn from earlier work

Prior documents recorded that enclosing a waste area triggers RCRA Subpart CC, and that an ADEQ No Exposure certification was available as a lever. Neither holds: Subpart CC attaches to the container and its vapour concentration rather than to any structure above it, and No Exposure is facility-wide and requires a storm-resistant shelter that an open-sided canopy cannot provide. The two positions also contradicted each other.

Open-sided remains the right design — on occupancy and fire classification, point-source air, heat, and wind uplift. The conclusion survives; the reasoning behind it did not.

A lever with no capital cost

Boxes arrive nailed to pallets — nominally 5 or 6, sometimes more than 10. The over-nailed units are what make ground-level separation slow and hazardous.

The packaging is prepared by the generators — Amazon, Target, Home Depot — so it cannot be mandated. What can be done costs almost nothing: publish an SOP and route it through regional sales managers, who forward it to their accounts as a request. A documented ask with a stated reason, travelling through a relationship that already exists.

Ask: banding or strapping where possible; where nails are used, a hard cap of six and none through the base rail. Partial adoption would reduce the highest-nail units.

Safety & cycle time

Two daily operations carry recurring, predictable injury exposure — not freak events. Both also hide the largest block of wasted cycle time on site.

Two operations, two different bottlenecks

Drums are shuttle-bound. Emptying one pallet of drums costs roughly 16 forklift round trips — two drums at a time, 40-60 yards each way, propane-assisted lift and dump at the far end. The laborer stands idle waiting through most of it. The constraint is not dump speed; it is a shuttle repeated sixteen times per pallet. A conveyor does not need to out-dump a forklift — it needs to delete the shuttle.

Boxes are labor-bound. One pallet moves per forklift trip, so the shuttle is a smaller share. The burn is manual: prep and liner unwrap, de-nailing, folding every box by hand, stacking, and wrapping. That is what the pallet station attacks.

Both arguments stand independently of the trailer-throughput question on the Overview tab.

Proposed workflow

Two redesigns. Between them they remove a full round trip, an entire warehouse operation, sixteen forklift shuttles per pallet, and four of the seven hazards below.

Boxes — de-nail at the dump point

Why it works: the cardboard only needs structural integrity until the liner is out. It is not strong on its top or sides, and it shifts as the liner falls. The moment the liner drops into the roll-off, the box has no remaining job.

  • Prep, dump and shake as today — liner falls into the 40-yard roll-off
  • De-nail right there, at the bin
  • Cardboard and pallets set straight into ground-level stacks on the spot
  • Collect in bulk, on a schedule, in few large moves

Eliminates: the 40-60 yd return trip carrying cardboard that never needed to travel, and the warehouse de-nailing operation entirely.

Drums — close the loop at bin height

The roll-off stands about 10 ft. Put the conveyor at that height and the drum never needs a forklift.

  • Conveyor runs at bin height (~10 ft)
  • Drums are tripped to tip into the roll-off in line
  • Tipped back upright onto a return conveyor
  • Conveyed to a funnel that self-nests them into the 8-stack
  • Nested stacks conveyed back near the dock ramp
  • Forklift moves finished pallets into the trailer

Current flow for comparison: 4 drums to staging at a time on the pallet, then 2 at a time from staging to the roll-off, then carried back to manual nesting. The 2-at-a-time legs are where throughput collapses, and they are what the conveyor removes.

Eliminates: 16 forklift round trips per pallet, the overhead toss, the 25% rework, and the laborer standing in the forklift path.

The one operation still unsolved

Everything else in the drum loop is addressed. Not yet designed: banding the nested stacks and placing them on pallets — today two bands per pallet at drum #2 and #6, 120 seconds each, pulled by hand around four stacks at height.

Banding cannot be eliminated. The stacks ride a 53 ft trailer to another site and are unloaded there. Road vibration works on them the whole way, and an unrestrained nest becomes an unloading hazard at the far end. A pallet collar does not travel with the load. At minimum one wrap of heat-sealed strap or a light ribbon is required.

Where the funnel discharges — a real trade

Both options share the front half: bin-height conveyor, trip bar, tip back upright, funnel self-nests into stacks of 8. They differ in what happens next.

The real distinction is coupling. B is shorter, cheaper, and removes a travel leg. A is more resilient, because the conveyor buffers two operations that will not always run at the same rate. With staff rotating every third day and load composition varying trailer to trailer, that buffer has value — but B decouples in the labour dimension, which is the variability actually lived with.

Decidable with a tape measure. If a palletizer and banding station fit at the funnel without crowding the dump point, B is likely better. If it is tight, the conveyor buys the space back.

Pallet station — proposed design

Today

  • Boxes nailed to pallets, separated on the ground
  • Worker bends, twists, moves the pallet, folds cardboard
  • Over-nailed units (10+) are very difficult
  • Empty pallets moved ad hoc — forklift-dropped or hand-carried

Proposed — zero-lift, one operator

The box and pallet arrive from the roll-off still nailed together, at ground level. That assembly is what this station receives.

  • Robotic arm or custom lifter raises the pallet from floor to waist level
  • Nails popped from the back — pry, punch, or air — on foot-pedal actuation, hands clear of the pinch point
  • Arm raises each pallet into the stack, positions 1 through 10
  • Level 10-high stack pushed out onto a ground conveyor
  • Forklift touches finished stacks only, never individual pallets

Payload class confirms it is buildable. An empty pallet plus box is roughly 130 lb — precisely where a cobot or light custom lifter works, and the same class that is 2.7 to 3.6 times too small for the dump line. The dump line wants hydraulics; this station wants a robot. Two different machines, each justified on its own terms.

Field data capture

Scheduling trailers by load type and packing roll-offs to weight both depend on knowing what is coming. Today that information is not captured until the material is already on the ground.

The capture loop

  1. Operator parks, forks down, and speaks the trailer number and load mix
  2. The message is parsed into structured fields — trailer, mix, drum and pallet counts
  3. The parsed values come back for a one-tap confirmation
  4. A row is appended to a sheet shared with the receiving facility
  5. The later reload message matches on trailer number and updates that row

Step 3 is not optional. Unconfirmed parsing of field messages produces data nobody trusts, and untrusted data is ignored within a month. The confirmation is what makes the sheet authoritative enough for another site to plan against.

Step count decides whether this survives contact with a busy shift. Under time pressure operators skip long sequences, and partial capture is worse than none.

Three constraints to settle before specifying

  • Device use while operating. Under 29 CFR 1910.178 the truck must be stopped with forks down before any device use. Write it into the SOP as a rule, not a habit.
  • Environment. Constant mast vibration plus a Somerton summer on a sunlit mount exceeds consumer tablet operating temperature. Rugged vehicle-mount hardware is the correct class.
  • Electrical area classification. If any part of the yard is classified, a standard tablet is an ignition source and the device must be intrinsically safe. Verify first

Manifests are the larger prize

Roughly 72 manifests per load, ranging 55–95, arrive with contents and weights unknown until the material is handled. Parsing them before arrival unlocks two things this proposal already depends on: scheduling trailers by load composition, and packing roll-offs to weight rather than to volume.

Effort depends entirely on format. A structured feed from the generator is trivial; digital documents are moderate; paper requires someone to photograph 55–95 pages per load, which is its own labour problem. Establish the format before estimating anything.

Knowing the load before it arrives

Every inbound trailer already carries a complete declaration of what is on it. Today that declaration is read after the trailer is on the dock. Read before, it decides which trailer to pull, which bay to hold, and how many operators to assign.

The data is already ours

Two real inbound packets were examined — trailers 548381 and 537464, roughly 50 manifests each. Every manifest in both packets is issued on our own registrant number and prepared by our retail services group in Peachtree Corners.

Home Depot, Target, Amazon and Walgreens are the legal generators — the waste is theirs. The typed record is ours. No data request to a retail customer is required to build this, which removes the slowest and least certain dependency from the plan.

Scanning the paper versus taking the upstream source

Reading these packets by scanner recovers the container type 96% of the time, the weight 80% of the time, and the container count only 52% of the time. The count is the field that plans the load, and it sits in the one box a scan renders worst.

That effort would also be wasted. Every field on these forms is machine-printed; only the signatures are handwritten. The documents are printed from a system that already holds the data cleanly. The task is a data-access request inside our own business, not a document-reading project.

Two things this exposes

The 45-minute phase does not reconcile with the container count. At roughly 134 containers moved two at a time, the staging-to-roll-off leg is 67 round trips. Against 45 minutes that is 40 seconds per trip including a 50-yard drive, which is not achievable. Either more than one operator runs that leg, or the phase figure is wrong, or the container estimate is high. The cycle-time case rests on this figure, which has not been measured.

Trailer weight does not reconcile with roll-off count. An estimated 15,000–17,000 lb per trailer against roughly 1.3 roll-offs per trailer does not square. The likely explanation is that manifest weight counts waste only, with liner and packaging adding the difference — but that is a hypothesis, not a finding.

What must be established

  • Does the packet arrive before the trailer? If it travels in the cab, no system fixes that — load and data arrive together, and pre-arrival planning is impossible from this source
  • Which internal system prints the manifests, and who can produce the same information for many trailers at once
  • Whether other origin points send the same packet structure
  • Whether these state-regulated non-RCRA wastes appear in the federal electronic manifest system, which would be a second route to the same data

What it unlocks

Dock allocation. Drum-heavy trailers pulled first, bays held, roll-off staged close, two operators assigned — freeing bays later in the day for concurrent unloading.

Density routing. Knowing container mix and weight before the dump decision lets a roll-off reach its weight and volume ceiling together instead of one or the other.

Arrival scheduling. Sequencing each trailer into the window that suits its composition.

What this figure depends on

The disposal saving rests on a price structure of $350 per pull plus about $0.025 a pound above 10,000 lb. Both numbers came from conversation; neither has been checked against a billing document. Stripped to its mechanism, the saving is 822 avoided trips multiplied by a $100 residual between a fixed per-pull fee and the value of a free tonnage allowance.

If the landfill instead bills per ton on full weight with a minimum charge — the most common commercial structure — the saving is zero, because the same tonnage moves either way and a linear rate does not care how many trips carry it. That is why the headline is stated as a range.

An itemised invoice would show the rate basis, any minimum charge, the annual tonnage and the pull count. The other sections of this deck do not rest on this figure.

No build cost is claimed here, because none has been quoted. Why a delay is never free here

Drivers run a Monday–Saturday schedule at more than ten hours a day, and cross forty hours by Thursday. From that point the week contains no straight-time hours at all.

The marginal rate is $42, not $28

A common objection to any delay-reduction claim is that the driver was on the clock regardless. The payroll pattern here differs. By Thursday he is on the clock at time and a half, so a trailer that is not ready, a load switched late, or a problem at the dock is not absorbed by an idle hour — it is purchased at the highest rate on the payroll.

Base $28.00/hr. Overtime $42.00/hr. Any model that values driver time saved at the base rate understates it by half.

Why there is no schedule today

The absence of a schedule is not an oversight. A schedule cannot be built without knowing what is on the trailers. With no view of contents there is no way to say which load unloads quickest, which carries something needed today, or how long a bay will be occupied — so trailers are worked in the order they arrive, and drivers park where they can.

The constrained resource is not the dock — it is the supply of empty trailers

An intercompany driver's objective is to drop a loaded trailer, hook an empty, and return for the next run. An empty exists only once a trailer has finished unloading. Working the shortest loads first therefore produces empties soonest, and a driver who can hook immediately does not wait at all.

This is not a preference. Sequencing shortest-first is the rule that minimises average waiting time across a set of jobs. It has not been applied here for one reason: you cannot sort by shortest until you know which is shortest — and that is precisely what the manifest declares before the truck moves.

The honest limit: shortest-first minimises average wait, not total unload time, and left unchecked it defers long loads indefinitely. Any schedule built this way must carry a deadline rule so a difficult trailer cannot be pushed back forever.

The stock-out case — one example of the cost of not knowing

Pomona supplies roll-off liners. If the site runs short, the trailer carrying replacements is already in the yard — but there is no way to know which one it is. Every hour spent without liners is an hour the dump line cannot run, and the remedy is sitting fifty yards away behind an unmarked door.

This is a stoppage, not a labour cost. Frequency and duration have not been measured, so no figure is claimed here.

Two conditions on the numbers above

The two labour levers are not the same hours and must not be merged. The 274 truck-hours freed by heavier roll-off fill belong to the landfill run and are redeployed to trailer spotting, not cashed. The delay hours in the table above belong to drivers waiting at the dock and are cashed. They add together only if they are different people — which has not been confirmed.

The $60,000 is provisional. It rests on a landfill tariff reconstructed from partial figures. Verified receipts are being retrieved and will move it in one direction or the other.

The 411 truck-hours can be taken as cash or redeployed — not both. One treatment is to take the disposal saving as cash and redeploy the hours to trailer spotting, which is what the dual-ramp case depends on. A model that booked the labour saving and the throughput it paid for would be double-counting, and is stated here so that it is not.
